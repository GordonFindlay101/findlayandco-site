How Incoterms Affect Your Customs, GST and Insurance in New Zealand

The Incoterm on your contract decides who clears the goods through New Zealand Customs, who pays the duty and GST, who can claim the GST back, and whether anyone has to insure the cargo. It does not change how Customs values the goods.

By Gordon Findlay, Findlay and Co · Reading time about 10 minutes

Incoterms decide who handles customs clearance at each end, who pays duty and GST, and who has to insure the goods. New Zealand law decides how duty and GST are calculated. The two most common outcomes for a New Zealand importer: under every rule except DDP you are the importer of record and claim the GST back, and only CIF and CIP oblige the seller to insure.

Most disputes about customs costs, GST credits and uninsured damage come back to a term that was chosen without anyone checking those three questions. This guide works through each one for importers and exporters, using Incoterms 2020 and the current New Zealand rules under the Customs and Excise Act 2018 and the GST Act.

For the background on all 11 rules, start with Incoterms 2020 explained for New Zealand importers and exporters, or keep the quick reference to the 11 Incoterms 2020 rules open alongside this page.

What do Incoterms decide about customs, GST and insurance?

Each Incoterms 2020 rule allocates export clearance, import clearance and any insurance obligation to the seller or the buyer. It allocates the task and the cost. The amount of duty and GST comes from New Zealand law.

Every rule has the same set of obligations, numbered A1 to A10 for the seller and B1 to B10 for the buyer. Articles A7 and B7 deal with export and import clearance. Articles A5 and B5 deal with insurance. Those two pairs are where the customs, GST and insurance consequences of your term come from.

The Incoterm does not say what the Customs value is, what tariff rate applies, whether the goods need an MPI permit or when title passes. Those sit with the Customs and Excise Act 2018, the GST Act 1985, the Biosecurity Act 1993 and, for the contract itself, the Contracts and Commercial Law Act 2017. A term can put a job on a party who cannot legally do it, which is the DDP problem covered below.

The seller clears export under ten of the eleven rules. The buyer clears import under ten of the eleven rules. Only CIF and CIP require insurance.

Does the Incoterm change the duty you pay in New Zealand?

No. New Zealand assesses duty on the Customs value of the goods, broadly the transaction value on an FOB type basis, and that value excludes international freight and insurance whichever term you buy on.

The Customs value is the price paid or payable for the goods, with certain additions such as some royalties and assists, and with international freight and insurance left out. Duty is the tariff rate for the goods' classification applied to that value. Many goods enter New Zealand duty free, either under the tariff or under a free trade agreement where the goods meet the rules of origin.

The term matters for the paperwork. A FOB or FCA invoice is already close to the Customs value. A CIF, CIP, DAP or DDP price includes freight and often insurance, so your customs broker needs those amounts shown separately to take them out. If the invoice gives one bundled figure, the broker may have to declare more than the true Customs value, and you pay duty on the freight as well. Ask the seller to show freight and insurance as separate lines on the commercial invoice.

Free trade agreement preference is a separate question from the Incoterm. Whoever is the importer of record has to hold the evidence of origin the agreement requires, so the seller needs to supply it under any term.

How is GST on imported goods calculated?

GST at 15 percent is charged on the Customs value plus any duty plus the cost of international freight and insurance. That base is the same under every Incoterm.

The GST base is wider than the duty base because it adds the freight and insurance back in, along with the duty itself. New Zealand Customs collects the GST at the border together with any duty, and Customs and MPI goods levies are charged on the import entry as well. Customs collects duty and GST at the border on consignments valued over NZ$1,000. On goods valued at NZ$1,000 or less sold to New Zealand consumers, GST registered overseas suppliers have charged GST at the point of sale since 1 December 2019.

Because the GST base includes freight and insurance whoever paid for them, a cheaper freight deal lowers your GST slightly and a CIF price does not lower it at all. The term changes who writes the cheque at the border, and that decides who can claim the GST back.

Who is the importer of record, and who claims the GST back?

The party responsible for import clearance under the Incoterm is the importer of record. Under ten of the eleven rules that is you, so you lodge the entry, pay the GST and claim it back as an input tax credit if you are GST registered.

The importer of record is the party named on the import entry and legally responsible to New Zealand Customs for the declaration, the duty and the GST. A business importing goods worth more than NZ$1,000 needs a Customs client code and a supplier code, and entries are lodged electronically through the Trade Single Window. Most importers have a customs broker or freight forwarder lodge them.

DDP changes this. Under DDP the seller must clear the goods for import and pay the duty and GST, so the seller, or an agent it appoints, has to be the importer of record in New Zealand. An overseas seller with no New Zealand presence often cannot do that, and the goods sit at the port or the freight station while storage and demurrage build up. Where DDP does work, the GST credit belongs to whoever paid the import GST, which is the seller or its agent, unless the seller is registered here and charges you GST on the sale.

For most business to business imports, DAP gives you the delivered price you were after while keeping you as the importer of record. You control the entry and the GST credit stays with you. Using DDP without checking who can be the importer is one of the common Incoterms mistakes that cost New Zealand businesses money.

Who handles MPI biosecurity clearance?

The party responsible for import clearance under the Incoterm also arranges biosecurity clearance with the Ministry for Primary Industries. Under every rule except DDP that is the New Zealand buyer.

Biosecurity clearance runs alongside the Customs entry. Depending on the goods and their packaging, MPI may need an import permit, a phytosanitary or treatment certificate, an inspection, or treatment such as fumigation before release. Containers and wooden packaging are checked for contamination and pests. Goods that need inspection or treatment often go to an MPI approved transitional facility, and the costs fall on the importer.

The Incoterm cannot make these costs disappear. It can make sure the seller supplies the right certificates and packaging declarations before the goods ship, so write those document requirements into the contract. For the people who manage this work at the border, read about what a transitional facility is in New Zealand.

Which Incoterms require cargo insurance?

Only two. CIF requires the seller to buy minimum cover, Institute Cargo Clauses (C). CIP requires the seller to buy all risks cover, Institute Cargo Clauses (A). Both must be for at least 110 percent of the contract price.

Clauses (C) is a named perils policy. It responds to major events such as fire or explosion, the vessel stranding, sinking or capsizing, collision and general average. It does not cover handling damage, cargo shifting inside the container or leakage from other cargo. Clauses (A) covers all risks of loss or damage apart from listed exclusions. Exclusions under every set of clauses include improper packing, ordinary wear, inherent vice and delay.

Incoterms 2020 raised the CIP standard from Clauses (C) to Clauses (A) and left CIF at Clauses (C). The seller under CIF or CIP must give you an insurance document that lets you claim directly. When the certificate arrives, check that it is assigned or endorsed to you, that the sum insured is at least 110 percent, which clauses apply and that the voyage matches the bill of lading. For machinery, electronics or fragile goods, negotiate CIP or arrange extra cover of your own. Our guide to FOB vs CIF for New Zealand sea freight covers the CIF policy in more detail.

Where should your own insurance start?

At the point where risk passes to you under the Incoterm. That point is fixed by the rule, and it is often well before the goods reach New Zealand.

  • EXW: when the goods are made available at the seller's premises, before loading.
  • FCA: when the goods are handed to your carrier, at the seller's premises or another named place.
  • CPT and CIP: when the goods are handed to the first carrier in the origin country, even though the seller pays freight to New Zealand.
  • FAS: when the goods are placed alongside the vessel at the origin port.
  • FOB, CFR and CIF: when the goods are on board the vessel at the origin port.
  • DAP: when the goods arrive at the named place in New Zealand, ready for unloading.
  • DPU: once the goods are unloaded at the named place.
  • DDP: on delivery at the named place, cleared for import.

The common gap is a buyer on CPT, CFR or CIF who assumes the seller is covering the goods until they arrive because the seller pays the freight. Risk has already passed at origin. If your annual marine cargo policy starts only on arrival, or only on goods you ship yourself, damage at sea falls into the gap.

Relying on the carrier is not a substitute. A carrier's liability is limited by law and by the terms of carriage. New Zealand gives the amended Hague Rules (the Hague Visby Rules) the force of law through the Maritime Transport Act 1994. They apply where the bill of lading is issued in, or the voyage starts from, a country that has adopted them, or where the bill of lading says they govern, and they cap the carrier's liability at 666.67 units of account (special drawing rights) per package or 2 per kilogram, whichever is higher. Other countries apply their own regimes. Either way the cap is often far below the value of the goods.

What do New Zealand exporters need to check?

Under every rule except EXW you clear the goods for export from New Zealand. Under DDP you also have to clear them into the buyer's country and pay its duty and taxes.

Export clearance means lodging an export entry with New Zealand Customs where one is required, and arranging any MPI export certification your goods need, such as a phytosanitary certificate or an official assurance for food products. Under section 11 of the GST Act 1985, goods you export are zero rated for GST when you, the supplier, enter them for export and export them, generally within 28 days of the time of supply. Keep the export entry and the transport documents on file as evidence.

EXW is a poor fit for New Zealand exports. The overseas buyer cannot easily lodge a New Zealand export entry, and because the buyer exports the goods, not you, the sale can fall outside zero rating. Check the GST treatment with your accountant before quoting EXW. FCA at your premises gives the buyer the same freight control and keeps export clearance with you. If you are asked to quote DDP, find out first whether you can act, or appoint an agent to act, as importer in the destination country, and price the duty and taxes with a margin for change.

What should the contract and invoice show?

The rule, a precise named place and the edition, plus the cost breakdown and documents your broker and insurer will need.

  • The term written in full form, for example CIF Auckland, INCOTERMS 2020, or DAP buyer's warehouse, East Tamaki, Auckland, INCOTERMS 2020.
  • Freight and insurance shown as separate amounts on the commercial invoice, so the Customs value can be worked out.
  • Who the importer of record will be, and if the term is DDP, who the seller's New Zealand agent is.
  • The insurance standard if you want more than the Incoterm minimum, for example CIF with Institute Cargo Clauses (A).
  • The origin documents needed to claim free trade agreement preference.
  • Any MPI certificates, treatment records or packaging declarations the goods need.

The Incoterm is one clause. Title, payment terms and remedies for defective goods sit with the rest of the contract and, in New Zealand, the Contracts and Commercial Law Act 2017. Your forwarder and customs broker can check the wording against the shipment before you sign. If you want to know who does what in that chain, see freight forwarder vs customs broker vs 3PL.

Glossary of customs and insurance terms

Customs value
The value New Zealand Customs assesses duty on, broadly the price of the goods on an FOB type basis, excluding international freight and insurance.
Importer of record
The party named on the import entry and legally responsible to New Zealand Customs for the declaration, duty and GST.
Customs client code
The identifier New Zealand Customs gives an importer or exporter so its entries can be lodged and linked to it.
Input tax credit
GST a registered business has paid on its purchases, including import GST, that it claims back in its GST return.
Zero rating
GST charged at 0 percent on qualifying exported goods, so the exporter charges no GST but can still claim its input tax.
Institute Cargo Clauses (A), (B) and (C)
Standard marine cargo insurance terms. (A) is all risks, (B) covers more named perils than (C), and (C) is the minimum.
Transitional facility
An MPI approved site where imported goods can be held, inspected or treated before biosecurity clearance.

The short version

The Incoterm decides who clears the goods and who insures them. New Zealand law decides the duty and GST. Under every rule except DDP you are the importer of record and keep the GST credit, and only CIF and CIP make the seller insure, so check where risk passes and start your own cover there.

Frequently asked questions

Do Incoterms change how much duty I pay in New Zealand?

No. New Zealand assesses duty on the Customs value of the goods, broadly the transaction value on an FOB type basis, which excludes international freight and insurance. The Incoterm changes how the invoice price is built up, so ask the seller to show freight and insurance separately and your broker can declare the correct value.

How is GST on imported goods calculated in New Zealand?

GST at 15 percent is charged on the Customs value plus any duty plus the international freight and insurance. That base is set by law and is the same whichever Incoterm you buy on. Customs collects duty and GST at the border on consignments valued over NZ$1,000.

Who claims the GST back on an import?

The importer of record pays the import GST and, if GST registered, claims it back as an input tax credit. Under ten of the eleven Incoterms 2020 rules that is the New Zealand buyer. Under DDP it is the seller or its New Zealand agent, so the buyer usually cannot claim it.

Why is DDP a problem for imports into New Zealand?

DDP makes the seller clear the goods for import and pay duty and GST, so the seller must be, or appoint, the importer of record in New Zealand. Many overseas sellers cannot, and the goods can be held while storage and demurrage build up. DAP usually gives a delivered price while keeping the importer role and the GST credit with the buyer.

Which Incoterms require the seller to insure the goods?

Only CIF and CIP. CIF requires minimum cover under Institute Cargo Clauses (C) and CIP requires all risks cover under Institute Cargo Clauses (A), both for at least 110 percent of the contract price. Under the other nine rules neither party has to insure, but the party carrying the risk usually does.

When should my cargo insurance start as a New Zealand importer?

From the point where risk passes to you under the Incoterm. Under FOB, CFR and CIF that is when the goods are on board at the origin port. Under FCA, CPT and CIP it is when the goods are handed to the first carrier. Under DAP, DPU and DDP risk stays with the seller until delivery in New Zealand.

Who arranges MPI biosecurity clearance under Incoterms?

The party responsible for import clearance arranges biosecurity clearance as well, which is the New Zealand buyer under every rule except DDP. Inspection, treatment and transitional facility costs fall on the importer, so the contract should require the seller to supply the certificates and packaging declarations MPI needs.

What do New Zealand exporters need to know about Incoterms and GST?

Under every rule except EXW the New Zealand seller clears the goods for export. Goods the seller enters for export and exports, generally within 28 days of the time of supply, are zero rated for GST, so keep the export entry and transport documents. FCA at your premises usually works better than EXW for exports.
